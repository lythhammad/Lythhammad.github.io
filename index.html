Professional Self-Assessment

Introduction and Professional Goals

On completing the Computer Science program and putting together this ePortfolio, I saw for sure what I'm strong at: problem solving, writing clean code, and writing secure code. I want to work in the security field, with a second focus on software engineering. I do care about security and quality of code, and I want to carry that into my work. The capstone helped me reflect on what I've already built over the years, and what I could have done better. It reminded me of how far I've come and how much I could be doing now. That reflection prepared me for the working world because I can look back at my own work and improve it.

Collaborating in a Team Environment

With XPLR I learnt how to work as a team. There were 5 developers, including me, working on the app, we split the back end and front end as evenly as possible, and decided who would work on what based on where we had the least experience so we could each develop a weaker skill. On the back end and front end I was in charge of the database and user aspects. We all worked together on the favourites database, then the rest was alone or in teams of two or five. When someone needed help they would ask a partner who was most skilled in that area.

I worked on (also at Hack Reactor): CarCar (full stack dealership website) – Built this project with another fellow full stack developer. We equally divided the back end and front end work. Whenever we had a discrepancy, we presented the argument to an instructor to help us choose the best approach for the project. Working on these two projects, I gained experience in sharing it, talking to people early, and ensuring that my work integrated well with my partners'.

Communicating with Stakeholders

Our audience on XPLR and CarCar was our instructors. 80% of our communication was of the form of Zoom meetings where we showed our code and explained our thought process when we encountered problems. We used Discord or Slack 20% of the time to send each other small snippets of code, or screenshots of completed features. On XPLR's final presentation, every member of our team gave a small 10-minute presentation on what they had built, what it does, and what we as a team did to make it work, to an audience of around 50 classmates plus 10 instructors and assistants. Afterwards I fielded some questions about my construction of the login UI, and received mostly positive feedback about how it looked and how well it fit into the project. This helped me learn to explain my work and field questions about it in a larger audience.

Data Structures and Algorithms

Hack Reactor. The course was more about practice and less theory, which is the part where I learned algorithms and Big O notation. I've used sorting algorithms, trees, and arrays and I've used sorting algorithms a lot at XPLR. Remember, with XPLR, memory was an issue as the APIs we used to load the sites gave us a ton of data. We couldn't have the site load forever, so we did some testing with data structures and algorithms to find the data we were interested in with the least amount of work possible.

Software Engineering and Databases

The software engineering skills I have are equally from SNHU and Hack Reactor. At SNHU, with the theory and review of code, I explored the SDLC and design patterns. When working on projects in Hack Reactor, I brought some of that knowledge into practice. I've utilized PostgreSQL in XPLR, MongoDB in CarCar, and MongoDB in Travlr at SNHU. Working with both relational and document databases I learned that the right choice of database depends on the application. But the most significant decision I've made is when I hardened the XPLR favorites feature to make sure a user can add favorites with a database constraint rather than just application logic. A rule enforced by the database. I now utilize that lesson in my designs.

Security

Most of what I know about security is from my time at SNHU but a little is from Hack Reactor. I've learned about all aspects of secure coding, security vulnerabilities, encryption, and network threats. In IT 340: Network and Telecommunication Management and CS 405: Secure Coding I learned that old code is often unsafe and should be replaced with new code. CS 499 took that lesson and applied it to a real-world codebase. I took an old project of mine called XPLR, and looked at the code to bring it up to current quality levels. In doing so I limited the wildcard CORS rules to only the methods and headers that the app needed, and added input validation to the account code. I also added error handling that logs the details of errors while only sending generic error messages to clients. This required me to view code from an attacker's point of view and patch the holes.

My Artifacts

These artifacts are three augmentations to XPLR, a full-stack national parks explorer I designed and built during a Hack Reactor program in 2023. The app allows users to explore parks all over the Americas; browse the parks, map, and weather; create a user account; save their favorite parks; and review parks. Each artifact enhances a different aspect of the same codebase.

  • Software Design and Engineering: I examined the backend API (main.py, authenticator.py, and the accounts router) and corrected the CORS settings, added input validation, error handling, and logging, and made the token endpoint return a predictable output.
  • Algorithms and Data Structures: I have refactored reviews query module to eliminate duplicate row-to-dictionary logic, for filtering park, sorting by rating and date, as well as an average rating calculated, which is executed in SQL.
  • Databases: I prepared the layer with error handling and logging, a unique constraint added to the favorites table through migration, and pagination. I added the same limit to the query which returned to all the favorite places in the database.

The first error-handling pattern was carried over into the remaining two enhancements, and the data-processing pattern from the second was carried over into the third. They illustrate how I evolve working code to be safer, leaner, and more maintainable.
